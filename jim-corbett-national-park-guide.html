1. History of Jim Corbett National Park

Long before "wildlife tourism" was a phrase anyone used, a hunter-turned-conservationist named Jim Corbett was arguing that the tigers of the Kumaon hills were worth more alive than dead. In 1936, the government of the time agreed, and Hailey National Park — India's very first national park — was born, carved out of the Patli Dun valley along the Ramganga river.

The park was renamed Jim Corbett National Park in 1957 in honour of the man himself, and in 1973 it earned a second, even bigger place in history: it became the launch site of Project Tiger, the national programme that anchors India's tiger conservation efforts to this day. The reserve now spans roughly 1,288 sq. km of sal forest, riverine grassland (locally called chaur), and Himalayan foothill, split into a strictly protected core zone and a more accessible buffer zone.

Did you know? Jim Corbett himself was originally a hunter hired to track down man-eating tigers and leopards that had killed hundreds of people in the Kumaon region — before turning conservationist and campaigning for the park that now bears his name.

3. How Many Vehicles & Guests Are Allowed Per Day?

This is one of the most common questions we get, and the forest department publishes fairly specific numbers. Across the main day-visit zones, roughly 180 jeeps are permitted to enter Corbett per day, split across the morning and afternoon shifts:

*Vehicle quotas are set and periodically revised by the forest department based on scientifically calculated carrying capacity — always confirm current numbers at the time of booking.

Doing the maths on the day-visit zones alone, that puts the realistic day-visitor ceiling at roughly 1,000-1,100 guests per day across Bijrani, Jhirna, Dhela, and Durga Devi combined — before adding Dhikala's separate night-stay and Canter allocation. Only two safari shifts run per day, morning (roughly 6:00-9:30 AM) and afternoon (roughly 2:00-5:30 PM), and private vehicles are never allowed inside — every visitor travels in an officially registered jeep or Canter with a naturalist guide.

4. Where to Stay: Inside the Forest vs Ramnagar Resorts

There are really two very different ways to stay near Corbett:

Inside the Core Forest — Official Forest Rest Houses

A small number of government-run Forest Rest Houses (FRHs) sit inside the reserve itself — Dhikala, Gairal, Sarapduli, and Khinnanauli among them. These are basic, no-frills lodges available only to guests with a valid night-stay permit, and they're the only way to legally spend a night inside the core zone.

Outside the Reserve — Ramnagar & Dhikuli Resorts

Ramnagar and the neighbouring Dhikuli belt host well over 300 hotels and resorts across every budget — from simple riverside guesthouses to five-star luxury properties, many with pools, spas, and their own private jeeps for zone transfers. For most travellers, this is where you'll actually be staying, with the safari itself booked separately for whichever zone you choose.

5. Wildlife You Can Spot in Jim Corbett

Corbett Tiger Reserve holds the highest tiger count of any single reserve in India — 260 tigers within the core area according to the 2022 All India Tiger Estimation — alongside an estimated 1,100+ wild Asian elephants and more than 600 recorded bird species.

  • Big cats: Royal Bengal Tiger, leopard, and occasionally the elusive fishing cat near water bodies.
  • Large mammals: Asiatic elephant, sloth bear, Himalayan black bear, Gaur (Indian bison), and Sambar deer.
  • Common sightings: Chital (spotted deer), langur, jungle cat, mongoose, wild boar, and barking deer.
  • Reptiles: Mugger and gharial crocodiles along the Ramganga river, plus several snake species.
  • Birdlife: Over 600 species, making Durga Devi and Pakhro (in the Sonanadi Range) especially popular with dedicated birders.

6. Rules & Regulations You Must Follow

The Corbett Tiger Reserve enforces its rules strictly under the Wildlife Protection Act, and violations can mean fines or an immediate end to your safari. Here's the honest do's-and-don'ts list:

Do

  • Carry your original ID document at all times (Aadhaar/Voter ID/Driving Licence for Indians, Passport for foreign nationals).
  • Book permits 45 days in advance (90 days for foreign nationals).
  • Stay seated inside the vehicle at all times.
  • Follow your naturalist guide's instructions.
  • Carry back all non-biodegradable waste with you.

Don't

  • Get off the vehicle or attempt to walk/trek (except in a few approved areas like Sitabani).
  • Carry non-vegetarian food into the reserve.
  • Blow horns, play music, shout, or attempt to feed animals.
  • Enter the park after sunset — night safaris are not permitted.
  • Expect a refund or transfer — permits are strictly non-refundable and non-transferable.

7. Best Time to Visit Jim Corbett

Jim Corbett stays open mid-November through June in most zones, with the core zones closing for monsoon from 1 July to mid-November for road safety and the breeding season. Within the open season: November to February is cooler and scenic but tigers are more dispersed, while March to June gives the best sighting odds as the heat forces animals toward waterholes — Jhirna and Dhela remain open through the monsoon as your only options if you're travelling off-season.

8. How to Reach Ramnagar

Ramnagar, the town this entire park is booked and administered from, sits right at the edge of the reserve. The nearest railhead is Ramnagar Railway Station itself, well connected to Delhi by direct trains. The nearest airport is Pantnagar (roughly 80 km away), with Delhi's IGI Airport being the more commonly used gateway for a road transfer of about 5-6 hours.
